01 · Overview

What is Homeostasis?

The process by which organisms regulate their internal environment to maintain conditions within tolerable limits — essential for enzyme function and metabolism.

🏠 Analogy

Homeostasis is like a smart thermostat in your house. The thermostat detects temperature (stimulus), the controller compares it to the set point, and the heater or AC turns on (effector) to bring temperature back to normal. Your body runs thousands of these loops simultaneously.

🎯

Stimulus-Response Model

Any change in the environment (stimulus) → detected by receptor → signal sent to control centre → effector responds → environment returns to set point.

🔄

Negative Feedback

The most common control system. The response opposes the original change, restoring equilibrium. Example: rising blood glucose triggers insulin release to lower glucose.

⚡

Nervous vs Endocrine

Nervous system: fast, electrical, short-lived, targeted. Endocrine system: slower, chemical (hormones), longer-lasting, broadcast via blood.

🧪

Why It Matters

Enzymes have narrow optimal ranges (pH, temp). Deviating outside these limits denatures enzymes, halting metabolism. Homeostasis keeps conditions in the "Goldilocks zone".

01b · Positive Feedback

Positive Feedback Loops

Unlike negative feedback, positive feedback amplifies the original stimulus — pushing the system further away from the starting point until a dramatic endpoint is reached, then stopping.

🎤 Analogy

Positive feedback is like microphone feedback. A small sound enters the mic → speaker amplifies it → mic picks it up again → even louder → screech! It escalates until someone removes the mic (the endpoint). In biology, the "someone removing the mic" is the completion of the event — e.g. the clot forms and stops the bleeding, or the baby is born and oxytocin drops.

✅ Negative Feedback

Response opposes the stimulus. Returns system to set point. Used for ongoing regulation (temperature, blood glucose, osmolarity). Most common homeostatic mechanism.

⟵ Dampens the change ⟵

⚡ Positive Feedback

Response amplifies the stimulus. Pushes system further from starting point. Used when a rapid, all-or-nothing outcome is needed. Always has a definite endpoint.

⟶ Amplifies the change ⟶

🩸 Blood Clotting (Coagulation Cascade)

Each clotting factor activates more clotting factors — a classic positive feedback cascade. Click "Injure Vessel" to trigger it.

Ready. Click "Injure Vessel" to begin the coagulation cascade.

How the cascade works:

  1. Vessel damage → exposes collagen → platelets adhere and release chemical signals.
  2. Signals activate clotting factors (a cascade of ~12 proteins).
  3. Each activated factor activates more of the next factor — amplification.
  4. Prothrombin → Thrombin (key enzyme) → Fibrinogen → Fibrin mesh forms.
  5. Platelets trapped in fibrin mesh → stable blood clot (endpoint reached).
  6. Clot formation stops the positive feedback. Anticoagulants (e.g. antithrombin) prevent runaway clotting.

👶 Childbirth — Oxytocin Positive Feedback

Uterine contractions trigger more oxytocin, causing stronger contractions — until the baby is born (endpoint). Click "Begin Labour" to simulate.

Ready. Click "Begin Labour" to start the oxytocin feedback loop.

The oxytocin loop:

  1. Baby's head presses on cervix → stretch receptors activated.
  2. Nerve signals → hypothalamus → posterior pituitary releases oxytocin.
  3. Oxytocin reaches uterus → stronger contractions.
  4. Stronger contractions push baby harder → more oxytocin released — positive feedback!
  5. Endpoint: baby is born → cervix no longer stretched → oxytocin drops → loop ends.

Three Types of Neurons

📡

Sensory Neurons

Carry signals FROM receptors TOWARD the central nervous system (afferent). Detect changes in the environment.

🔗

Interneurons

Located entirely within the CNS. Connect sensory to motor neurons, integrate and process information.

💪

Motor Neurons

Carry signals FROM CNS TO effectors (muscles/glands) — efferent. Produce the physical response.

🏄 Analogy

An action potential is like a row of falling dominoes. Once the first domino tips past the threshold, the chain reaction is unstoppable — all-or-nothing. Once fallen, each domino "resets" (refractory period) before it can fall again.

Phases of the Action Potential

💤

Resting Potential

Inside of axon is −70mV relative to outside. Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains this by moving 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.

📈

Depolarisation

Stimulus opens Na⁺ channels. Na⁺ rushes IN, making interior more positive (up to +30mV). Threshold: −55mV.

📉

Repolarisation

Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ channels open. K⁺ rushes OUT, restoring negative charge inside.

🔄

Refractory Period

Brief period when axon cannot fire again. Ensures impulse travels in ONE direction only.

05 · Synaptic Transmission

The Synapse

How nerve signals cross the gap between neurons using chemical neurotransmitters.

📬 Analogy

Synaptic transmission is like sending a letter across a river. The presynaptic neuron is the sender — it packages the message (neurotransmitter) into envelopes (vesicles) and launches them across the river (synaptic cleft). Receptors on the postsynaptic neuron are the mailboxes — only the right envelope fits the right mailbox, triggering signal transduction inside the cell.

🔬 Synapse Animation

Click "Transmit Signal" to watch neurotransmitter release across the synaptic cleft.

Ready. Click "Transmit Signal" to begin.

Steps of Synaptic Transmission

  1. Action potential reaches axon terminal of presynaptic neuron.
  2. Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters axon terminal.
  3. Vesicles containing neurotransmitters fuse with presynaptic membrane (exocytosis).
  4. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synaptic cleft (~20–40 nm gap).
  5. Neurotransmitters bind to specific receptors on postsynaptic membrane.
  6. Signal transduction: receptor binding changes ion channel state, generating EPSP or IPSP.
  7. Neurotransmitters removed: enzymatic breakdown (e.g. acetylcholinesterase) or reuptake.
06 · Endocrine System

Hormones & Cell Signalling

How chemical messengers travel through the blood to reach only target cells displaying specific receptors.

📻 Analogy

Hormones are like radio broadcasts. The signal goes everywhere, but only radios (cells) tuned to the right frequency (receptor type) actually receive the message. A cell's sensitivity depends on how many receptors it displays — more receptors = louder signal.

🩸 Hormone Circulation Animation

Watch hormones broadcast through the bloodstream — only cells with matching receptors respond.

Key Concepts

🎯

Specificity

Only cells with complementary receptors respond to a hormone. Lock-and-key principle applies.

📊

Sensitivity

More receptors displayed = greater sensitivity to the hormone. Cells can up- or down-regulate receptor numbers.

🔄

Signal Transduction

Receptor binding triggers an internal cascade of molecular changes, altering cell activity (e.g. enzyme activation, gene expression).

🟫

Brown Adipose Tissue

Structural. Specialised fat rich in mitochondria. Burns fat directly to produce heat (non-shivering thermogenesis). Common in infants and hibernating animals.

🧥

Insulation

Structural. Fur, feathers, blubber — trap air or create barriers to reduce heat loss. Fur erection (piloerection) increases insulating layer thickness.

🤝

Kleptothermy

Behavioural. Stealing/borrowing heat from another animal. E.g. pythons coiling around eggs; some fish sheltering in warm-bodied hosts.

🐻

Hibernation

Behavioural. Extended torpor during winter. Metabolic rate drops dramatically, body temp falls, breathing slows. Bears, ground squirrels, hedgehogs.

🐚

Aestivation

Behavioural. Summer equivalent of hibernation. Animals survive hot/dry conditions in a dormant state. Lungfish, snails, some amphibians.

😴

Torpor

Behavioural. Short-term daily metabolic suppression. Hummingbirds enter torpor each night to conserve energy, re-warming each morning.

🌬️

Evaporative Heat Loss

Physiological. Sweating, panting — water evaporating from skin/respiratory surfaces carries away large amounts of heat energy.

🔥

Thermogenesis

Physiological. Shivering (rapid muscle contractions produce heat) and non-shivering thermogenesis (brown fat uncoupling).

🩸

Vasomotor Control

Physiological. Adjusting blood vessel diameter. Vasodilation brings warm blood to skin surface (lose heat). Vasoconstriction reduces blood flow to extremities (retain heat).

08 · Osmoregulation

Water Balance & ADH

How the kidneys and ADH maintain blood osmolarity through negative feedback.

🚰 Analogy

ADH is like a dam controller. When the body detects the blood is too concentrated (dehydrated), ADH is released — like opening sluice gates in the kidney collecting duct. More water is reabsorbed, urine becomes concentrated, and blood returns to normal osmolarity.

Key Structures

🧠

Hypothalamus (Osmoreceptors)

Detects changes in blood osmolarity. When blood is too concentrated, signals posterior pituitary.

💉

ADH (Antidiuretic Hormone)

Released by posterior pituitary. Travels via blood to kidneys. Increases aquaporin channels in collecting duct.

🫘

Kidney (Collecting Duct)

More aquaporins = more water reabsorption = concentrated urine. Low ADH = dilute urine, high urine volume.

09 · Plant Water Balance

Water Regulation in Plants

Structural and hormonal mechanisms that maintain water balance in plants across different environments.

🌿 Plant Water Balance Simulation

Adjust soil water availability to see stomatal and hormonal responses.

Good water availability. Stomata open for gas exchange.
🌬️

Stomata

Pores in leaf epidermis. Guard cells control opening/closing. Open: CO₂ in, O₂ out, water vapour out (transpiration). Close under stress.

💧

Vacuoles

Large central vacuoles store water. Turgor pressure keeps plant cells rigid. When dehydrated, cells lose turgor → wilting.

🛡️

Cuticle

Waxy layer on leaf/stem surfaces. Waterproof barrier reducing water loss by evaporation. Thicker in xerophytes (dry-adapted plants).

🚨

Abscisic Acid (ABA)

Stress hormone. Released when water is scarce. Signals guard cells to close stomata, reducing further water loss through transpiration.

Glossary

Key Terms

Homeostasis
Maintenance of stable internal conditions within tolerance limits.
Negative Feedback
Control mechanism where the response opposes the original stimulus.
Positive Feedback
Control mechanism where the response amplifies the original stimulus, pushing toward a definite endpoint.
Coagulation Cascade
Positive feedback process where clotting factors amplify each other, resulting in a blood clot.
Oxytocin
Hormone driving uterine contractions in childbirth via positive feedback; also involved in bonding.
Neurotransmitter
Chemical messenger released across a synapse (e.g. acetylcholine, dopamine).
Action Potential
Electrical impulse generated when membrane potential reaches threshold (~−55mV).
Myelin Sheath
Fatty insulating layer speeding signal conduction via saltatory conduction.
Synapse
Junction between two neurons where signal is transmitted chemically.
Hormone
Chemical messenger produced by endocrine gland, transported via blood.
ADH
Antidiuretic hormone; increases water reabsorption in kidney collecting duct.
Vasodilation
Widening of blood vessels; increases blood flow to skin surface, losing heat.
Vasoconstriction
Narrowing of blood vessels; reduces blood flow to extremities, conserving heat.
Thermogenesis
Heat production by the body (shivering or non-shivering via brown adipose tissue).
Abscisic Acid (ABA)
Plant stress hormone triggering stomatal closure during water deficit.
Osmoregulation
Regulation of water and solute balance in body fluids.
Transpiration
Loss of water vapour from plant surfaces, mainly through stomata.
Signal Transduction
The cascade of molecular events following receptor binding that alters cell activity.
Nociceptor
Pain receptor detecting potentially damaging stimuli.
